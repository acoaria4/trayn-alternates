# Nurture by TRAYN Nutrition

## Product development discussion and working technical brief

**Date:** 17 October 2026  
**Status:** Concept for independent food-technologist and nutrition review  
**Scope:** Comprehensive record of the food-product discussion, including the hot-water/hot-milk clarification and the proposed milk-protein/whey direction. Website implementation is outside this scope.

**19 October follow-up:** The founder has selected an MPC-led dairy coffee bench trial for women aged 25–45, targeting 15 g protein from the powder, hot-water and hot-milk preparation, and an approximately INR45 recurring packed cost. The [trial formulation and testing plan](nurture-mpc-coffee-trial.md) contains the current 25 g serving proposal, 1 kg bench batch and provisional B12/D targets. The decision register below preserves the earlier 17 October discussion; the later trial remains subject to ingredient, nutritional, regulatory and performance validation.

Nurture is intended to be an enjoyable everyday protein beverage for women, with selected micronutrients and a convenient preparation routine. The founder wants it to work with both hot water and hot milk, taste good enough to take the place of a habitual tea or coffee occasion, and reach a broad adult customer base.

The present development hypothesis is a dairy-based powder built around milk protein and whey, with milk powder and possibly a small fat contribution selected for the desired drinking experience. This is a route to evaluate, not a completed formulation. No blend ratio, ingredient supplier, micronutrient dose, powder serving weight, preparation temperature, shelf life, manufacturing process, price or health claim has been approved.

The companion PDF is a short review brief for a food technologist. This Markdown preserves the fuller reasoning, options, distinctions and questions behind that brief. Technical suggestions below should be challenged and revised through formulation, nutritional, regulatory and consumer evaluation.

## 1. What the founder has asked for

- A protein-containing everyday drink, originally described as a whey protein drink.
- Great taste and an appealing daily experience, with the ambition to replace habitual tea or coffee occasions.
- Selected micronutrients with a defensible nutritional purpose.
- Preparation using **hot water as well as hot milk**.
- An initial audience of women aged **18-55**, with openness to changing the age positioning or product direction to reach an appropriate broad audience.
- Evaluation of a **protein plus milk-solids route**.
- A discussion and an independent technical evaluation before making product decisions. The website concept and packaging artwork do not establish a real formulation.

The clarification about hot water and hot milk is a product requirement. The suggested 25-45 marketing focus, protein range, one-cup daily occasion and specific formulation architecture are working proposals, not founder-approved specifications.

## 3. Product proposition and daily occasion

The opportunity is to make a drink that people choose because they enjoy the entire cup, with a useful nutritional contribution as part of that experience. Competing for a familiar tea or coffee occasion creates clear expectations: a pleasant aroma, satisfying temperature and mouthfeel, straightforward preparation, a reasonable cost per cup and no unpleasant final sips.

The initial proposal is to target **one daily beverage occasion**. Replacing every tea or coffee with a fully fortified serving would create a different intake pattern and needs separate evaluation. Micronutrient design should account for realistic use, other fortified foods and existing supplements.

Tea and coffee should not be treated as inherently unhealthy. The relevant comparison is the customer's actual current drink, including any milk and sugar. A protein beverage may supply more protein and energy than black tea or coffee; it should not automatically be positioned as a weight-loss product or assumed to have fewer calories.

A caffeine-free Nurture could compete on comfort, flavour and the daily ritual. It would not provide coffee's caffeine effect. If actual tea, coffee or caffeine-containing ingredients are considered later, their role, amount and implications for positioning must be made explicit.

This concept is not currently a meal replacement, a treatment for nutritional deficiency, a pregnancy product or a clinical nutrition formula. None of those roles should be inferred from the packaging design or the presence of protein and vitamins.

## 4. Audience and age positioning

The proposed strategy separates **the first marketing audience** from **the eventual suitable user population**. Women aged 25-45 could be a manageable first audience for positioning and research. That choice is a commercial hypothesis, not evidence that women outside those ages would not benefit or cannot use an appropriately formulated product.

A broad adult product may be feasible across the original 18-55 range and potentially beyond 55. The final formula and intended use need review before making an age suitability statement. There is no reason in this brief to impose a hard cutoff at 55 solely because the product contains protein.

Life stage and diet can matter more than age alone. Menstruation, blood loss, pregnancy, lactation, dietary patterns, existing nutrient intake and relevant health conditions influence the nutritional assessment. Iron requirements, in particular, are not uniform across all adult women. US NIH guidance illustrates life-stage differences; Indian formulation and labelling decisions need the applicable Indian references and rules. [R5, R9]

| Research group | What to learn | What not to assume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 18-24 | Taste, convenience, affordability and whether the drink fits existing habits | That younger women require a different protein source solely because of age |
| 25-39 | Repeat use, preparation routine, willingness to pay and relevant dietary gaps | That all customers have the same nutrient needs or are non-pregnant |
| 40-55 | Drinking experience, dietary context, menstrual/life-stage variation and tolerance | That all women in this bracket are postmenopausal |
| 55+ | Acceptability, portion preference, tolerance and appropriate nutritional positioning | That an upper age exclusion is necessary or that this is clinical nutrition |

Pregnancy and breastfeeding should receive a separate formulation and intended-use assessment. Children are outside the current adult concept. Medical nutrition needs should not be covered through a general suitability statement. A dairy-based product also cannot cover consumers with milk allergy simply by lowering lactose.

If the core benefit becomes iron provision for a particular need, the audience, dose and evidence requirements may need tighter definition. A broadly positioned base product and a later iron-focused product are options to assess, not a decision to launch multiple SKUs immediately.

## 5. Protein contribution and preparation basis

The discussion proposed exploring approximately **10-15 g of protein supplied by one serving of powder** in an initial **180-200 mL prepared cup**. These figures are starting points for feasibility and sensory work. They do not establish an optimal dose, a recommended daily intake, a minimum effective dose or a permitted label claim.

The amount of protein per cup must be separated from the weight of powder. A serving containing 12 g of protein will generally weigh more than 12 g once other ingredients are included. The final serving weight follows from the complete formula and desired concentration.

For trial interpretation, define the prepared beverage consistently. Record powder mass, liquid mass/volume, liquid type, mixing conditions and resulting prepared volume. Select one convention for the development specification instead of comparing differently diluted samples without acknowledging it.

As an approximate bench calculation, 10-15 g of protein in a 200 mL finished beverage corresponds to 5-7.5 g protein per 100 mL. This is arithmetic for experimental planning, not evidence of thermal stability or sensory acceptance at those concentrations.

Prefer one defined powder serving for both water and milk if technically and nutritionally appropriate. Liquid quantity may be adjusted after testing. Arbitrarily reducing powder in milk would also reduce the added micronutrient dose and change the product's intended daily contribution.

**Illustrative calculation only:** if a powder serving supplies 12 g protein and the selected quantity of milk supplies another 6 g, the prepared milk drink supplies 18 g; the water-prepared version supplies 12 g. These are hypothetical inputs. Actual values must use verified ingredient and preparation data. Milk fat, lactose, energy, calcium and any fortification also change the prepared drink's composition.

## 6. Why investigate milk protein with whey and milk solids

The preferred first investigation is a milk-protein/whey system with a controlled amount of milk powder and an optional small fat adjustment. The reason is the desired hot, comforting beverage experience, especially when prepared with water. The product does not have to contain only whey to meet that consumer occasion.

The ingredient terms must be distinguished:

| Ingredient | Potential role | Points for technical assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whey protein concentrate | Protein enrichment and adjustment of the protein balance | Supplier-specific protein, lactose, flavour, foam and thermal behaviour |
| Whey protein isolate | Concentrated whey protein where its composition or lower lactose contribution is useful | Grade, instant properties, flavour, functionality and actual cost; isolate does not automatically solve hot-cup performance |
| Milk protein concentrate | Concentrated milk proteins containing both casein and whey | Hydration, processing history, mineral balance, viscosity and heat behaviour |
| Skim milk powder | Dairy solids for the intended flavour/body and some protein | Lactose contribution, total solids, sweetness perception and protein efficiency |
| Whole milk powder | Dairy flavour plus fat and richness | Prepared-with-milk richness, energy and storage performance |
| A selected fat component | Possible improvement to creaminess if needed | Emulsion/dispersibility, oxidation, nutrition, labelling and whether the sensory benefit justifies it |

The US Dairy Export Council describes milk protein concentrate as retaining approximately milk's natural 80:20 casein:whey protein balance. It gives typical nonfat dry milk composition of 34-36% protein and about 52% lactose, compared with MPC80 at about 80% protein and 5-6% lactose. These are ingredient-reference examples, not purchasing specifications. Supplier certificates and compositional data must govern the formula. [R3]

An ingredient mass ratio is not a protein ratio. MPC already supplies both casein and whey, skim milk powder also contributes both, and whey ingredients differ in protein concentration. Calculate the total casein and whey contributions from all ingredients before describing the blend. No ratio has been selected for Nurture.

Research on UHT-treated high-protein liquid systems found that casein inclusion improved stability relative to whey-rich systems under the study's conditions. This gives a rationale for investigating blends; it does not prove instant dispersion or stability in a freshly heated household cup. Manufacturing conditions, composition and the use case differ. [R1]

Milk solids should be selected for a specific job rather than added indiscriminately. A large skim-milk-powder contribution may increase lactose and serving weight. A highly concentrated protein system may create different hydration and texture problems. The food technologist should identify the workable balance.

## 7. Two distinct engineering tasks: hydration and heat behaviour

### 7.1 Hydration

Assess whether powder wets, sinks, disperses and hydrates using a normal spoon. Floating rafts, dry centres and visible lumps can make an otherwise nutritious product inconvenient. Ingredient instantisation and agglomeration are relevant options, but the whole blend must be tested.

A study of WPI-fortified skim milk powder found improved wetting and flow properties after fluidised-bed agglomeration. It also found differences in the reconstituted product, including viscosity and colour. This supports evaluating the process, not assuming a universal result for Nurture. [R2]

A separate dairy-powder study found that ingredient incorporation mode and agglomeration influenced rehydration. This is a reason to discuss processing with a manufacturer rather than assume any dry blend behaves like a purpose-designed instant powder. [R4]

### 7.2 Heat behaviour

After hydration, the beverage should remain acceptably smooth over its intended drinking period. Temperature, exposure time, concentration, pH, minerals, ingredient history and mixing conditions belong in the test design. Heat-associated aggregation and mouth-drying are relevant risks in whey beverages; experimental sensory research supports assessing these properties explicitly. [R6]

The proposed consumer promise is hot preparation in both water and milk. It should not be translated into a claim of suitability for boiling, simmering, kettle preparation or prolonged hot holding without testing. A reconstitution claim and an on-stove cooking claim are different requirements.

The technologist should establish a feasible mixing-temperature window, method, portion and standing time. Test realistic variations in milk type and water composition. Aggressive high-temperature conditions may be useful as controlled laboratory stress tests; those temperatures are not recommendations for drinking.

Preparation instructions should never require shaking a hot beverage in a sealed container. Any consumer method must be practical, reproducible and assessed for safe use.

## 8. Sensory direction and flavour development

The desired experience is light-to-moderately creamy, aromatic and enjoyable through the final sip. It should fit a daily cup rather than rely on a heavy milkshake experience. Sweetness level and mouthfeel are formulation decisions to test, not automatic consequences of a women's nutrition positioning.

Two initial flavour hypotheses were discussed:

- **Cocoa with restrained vanilla:** a familiar comforting direction to explore.
- **Cardamom with vanilla/milk notes:** a warm beverage direction worth assessing with intended Indian consumers.

Neither has been selected or consumer validated. Cocoa, spice and flavour ingredients should be evaluated as part of the complete drink, including their effect on sediment, appearance, aftertaste and preparation. If iron is considered, evaluate its performance within that actual flavour matrix rather than assume flavour masking solves nutritional or stability issues.

Make a pleasant protein base first, then compare the same base after adding the proposed micronutrient premix. Taste, aroma and physical behaviour must be checked again. An acceptable unfortified base does not establish an acceptable fortified final product.

The sensory assessment should include:

1. Aroma on preparation and while drinking.
2. Initial sweetness and overall flavour balance.
3. Body, creaminess, foam and ease of swallowing.
4. Graininess, chalkiness, mouth-drying and mouth-coating.
5. Lingering sweetener, protein or mineral aftertaste.
6. Sediment and the taste of the last few sips.
7. Changes as the beverage stands and cools.
8. Prepared-in-water and prepared-in-milk versions separately.

Do not assume that eliminating all added sugar necessarily produces the best repeated-use drink. Compare feasible sweetness systems against the nutritional and regulatory brief, and assess the finished cup rather than a front-of-pack phrase.

## 9. Micronutrient strategy

The principle is a small, justified shortlist. An ingredient's biological role does not demonstrate that every customer needs more of it or that this product will improve a health outcome. Evaluate likely dietary gaps, intended use, baseline nutrients, permitted forms, dose, interactions, tolerance and retention.

| Candidate | Rationale discussed | Required decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamin B12 | Relevant to normal nutritional function; worth considering where customers consume limited animal-source foods | Need in the target diet, permitted form, dose, premix uniformity and stability; avoid an automatic energy-boost promise |
| Vitamin D | Established role in calcium handling and bone health supports considering it | Appropriate contribution, permitted form, carrier/distribution and stability; no generalised outcome claim from presence alone |
| Calcium | Could support a defensible nutritional contribution | Count protein-base and preparation-milk calcium first; evaluate the amount, form, physical stability and sensory effect of any addition |
| Iron | Potentially relevant to particular adult female needs | Separate population/need assessment, permitted form and dose, bioavailability, sensory/oxidative behaviour and repeat-use tolerance |
| Other B vitamins, zinc, magnesium, folate, biotin or broad antioxidant additions | Not selected as a routine full package | Include only with a specific nutritional and formulation rationale; the concept label is not a formula specification |

B12 and vitamin D are candidates because of established nutritional roles, not because a deficiency has been demonstrated in all intended customers. NIH fact sheets provide background on these roles and life-stage considerations. [R7, R8]

Calcium fortification changes the formulation system and merits its own stability assessment; literature identifies interactions between calcium salts and whey-based beverages. Ingredient choice and process conditions must be considered together. [R10]

Iron needs vary with life stage and individual circumstances. The broad 18-55 label should not be used as a reason to assign one substantial iron dose to everyone. A broad base formulation without a central iron promise is an option; a separately justified iron-focused direction is another. Neither inclusion nor exclusion has been finalised. [R5]

Tea polyphenols can affect non-haem iron absorption under specific meal conditions; a trial in young Indian women illustrates this interaction. That does not establish a health benefit from replacing tea with an untested Nurture formula. If tea ingredients or iron are contemplated, evaluate the actual matrix and evidence. [R11]

For every selected nutrient, the technologist/nutrition reviewer should specify:

- Why it belongs in this particular product and audience.
- Baseline contribution from all ingredients and the two preparation methods.
- Proposed elemental or vitamin-active amount, ingredient form and serving frequency.
- Relevant Indian reference values, upper-intake considerations and category limits.
- Permitted ingredient status, supplier specifications and premix quality.
- Blend uniformity, segregation risk and analytical verification.
- Processing, preparation and storage retention through the proposed shelf life.
- Tolerance, relevant interactions and plausible multiple-serving use.
- Any justified manufacturing overage and how compliance will be maintained.
- Which proposed claims can actually be supported by the finished product.

No micronutrient quantities have been recommended or approved in this discussion. US reference values should not be substituted automatically for Indian product requirements.

## 10. Tolerance, milk allergens and broad suitability

Whey and other milk proteins remain dairy allergens. Low-lactose and milk-allergy suitability are different matters. A formulation containing milk proteins should not be described as suitable for milk-allergic consumers. [R12]

The total lactose contribution comes from all ingredients and, for milk preparation, the added milk. Selecting whey isolate does not make a blend with skim milk powder and ordinary milk lactose-free. Any low-lactose positioning would require its own compositional specification, analytical evidence and regulatory review.

During development, evaluate repeated-use tolerance of the complete formulation, including the sweetness system and any optional fibres or other ingredients. This is a reason for a defined safety-reviewed trial protocol, not a basis for an unsupported 'easy to digest' claim.

The product should complement the user's overall diet. More protein or more micronutrients are not automatically appropriate for every consumer, and clinical needs require individual assessment outside the current general-food concept.

## 11. Initial prototype comparison

The following are experimental directions, not formulas or manufacturing instructions. Match protein contribution, prepared concentration and approximate sweetness where possible so the comparison is interpretable.

| Route | Architecture to investigate | Main learning goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Whey-led base with some skim milk powder | Can a relatively simple whey-led system meet both hot-cup and sensory requirements? |
| B | MPC plus whey, with a smaller milk-powder contribution | Does the milk-protein system give a better balance of hydration, stability, body and lactose contribution? |
| C | The better-performing base with a small fat/creaminess adjustment | Is there a worthwhile sensory gain without excessive richness in milk preparation? |

The technologist should choose ingredient grades, ratios, process and any permitted stabilisation system. If a whey-only baseline helps experimental interpretation, include it as a control. Do not select a fixed casein:whey ratio from a paper and treat it as a validated Nurture recipe.

Record ingredient specifications and processing histories. A supplier's generic ingredient name or headline protein percentage is insufficient to establish functional equivalence across lots or suppliers.

## 12. Validation and development plan

### Stage 1: Agree the development brief

Confirm market, age/life-stage positioning, powder format, intended frequency, protein contribution, cup basis, taste direction and target cost. Decide whether caffeine reduction is part of the product's purpose. Confirm who will own nutrition, regulatory and manufacturing decisions.

### Stage 2: Bench feasibility

Build the limited comparison set above. Test spoon preparation in water and milk separately. Record wetting, dispersion, foam, lumps, pH, appearance, viscosity/body, sediment and behaviour over the intended drinking period. Determine whether the brief requires direct hot reconstitution or a different method. Define measurable pass/fail criteria before selecting a winner.

### Stage 3: Fortified final-formula development

Evaluate the micronutrient shortlist and the complete nutrient balance. Add the selected premix to the best base and repeat sensory, preparation and physical-stability checks. Review powder homogeneity and segregation. Verify nutrients analytically where needed rather than relying only on spreadsheet addition.

### Stage 4: Safety-reviewed consumer evaluation

Use a suitable, safety-reviewed prototype and a defined participant protocol. Recruit across the proposed adult age groups, with appropriate eligibility and allergy screening. Evaluate the drink in its real intended context over repeated occasions, rather than relying solely on a central tasting session.

Measures should include preparation success, overall liking, whether it replaces a current cup, end-of-cup enjoyment, tolerance, use frequency, willingness to buy again and acceptable price. A small exploratory sample can inform iteration; it cannot prove broad health benefits or market demand.

### Stage 5: Pilot manufacturing and packaged shelf-life work

Confirm whether the intended manufacturer can achieve the required ingredient processing, instant properties, mixing uniformity and pack format. Review microbiological safety, contaminants/adulteration risks relevant to the ingredients, moisture/water activity, physical stability, flavour changes and nutrient retention under the proposed storage conditions. Set specifications and testing against the applicable category and risk assessment.

The tin in the concept artwork is a visual reference only. Packaging selection, barrier properties, food-contact suitability, closure, scoop/dosing accuracy, humidity exposure after opening and the in-use period need evaluation. No pack size or shelf life is committed by the mockup.

### Stage 6: Regulatory, label and release review

Confirm the applicable Indian category, ingredient/additive permissions, nutrient forms and levels, intended-use statements, allergen declarations, preparation nutrition and claim substantiation. Plan release specifications and continuing quality controls. The FSSAI framework and applicable amendments should be checked for the actual final product. [R13, R14]

## 13. Commercial and manufacturing questions

The target price per serving has not been supplied. No rupee cost target or manufacturing quote should be inferred from this brief. Ask for ingredient and process cost drivers before approving an architecture.

Compare cost **per defined powder serving and delivered protein contribution**, not just per kilogram of ingredient. Include the micronutrient premix, flavour system, processing/instantisation, quality testing, packaging, yield and supply consistency. Customer milk cost is a separate part of the prepared-cup experience.

The water route should be a serious sensory benchmark because it is an explicit requirement and avoids relying on customer milk to supply the missing body or taste. The milk route must then remain enjoyable at a practical dilution. If the same powder cannot meet both methods at an acceptable cost and serving size, the technologist should identify the compromise explicitly.

The product may ultimately be described more accurately as a milk-and-whey protein beverage than as pure whey. The final generic name and marketing language should follow the actual composition and category.

## 14. Triggers for a pivot

Consider revising the brief if:

- Acceptable hot-water taste requires a serving weight or ingredient cost customers will not accept.
- The product is pleasant in water but unacceptably rich in milk, or the reverse, without a practical preparation solution.
- Reliable spoon preparation requires an unrealistic routine for the intended daily occasion.
- The desired protein contribution produces unacceptable mouth-drying, foam, thickness or sediment.
- The proposed micronutrient package undermines sensory quality, stability or broad intended use.
- An iron-centred benefit becomes the priority and calls for a more specific audience or product role.
- Repeat-use research shows weak habit substitution or willingness to buy despite an appealing initial taste.
- A feasible manufacturer or appropriate shelf-life/quality system cannot support the proposed product.

Possible responses include adjusting the protein target, changing the protein system, choosing one primary preparation method, narrowing the nutritional purpose or reassessing format. These are decision options, not predetermined failures or approved pivots.
